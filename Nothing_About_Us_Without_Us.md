# Nothing About Us Without Us

## Inclusive research, lived experience and the ethical value of better data

**Double FW Ltd · Version 1.0 · 6 November 2026**  
**Companion guidance for the Data Ethics and Disability repository**

## 1. Start with accumulated knowledge, then involve the people affected

Governance documentation, including Double FW guidance, WCAG, the European Accessibility Act (EAA) and other established frameworks, draws on accumulated knowledge from projects, research programmes, data sources, professional practice and expert contributions. These resources carry forward learning about disabled people's experiences. In that sense, knowledge “about us”, including knowledge contributed by people with lived experience, is already embedded in the foundation.

These sources have different purposes: WCAG is a technical standard, the EAA is legislation, and Double FW resources provide practical and organisational guidance. Their development processes and the extent of direct community participation differ. Recognising their inherited knowledge must not be taken as proof that every affected community has shaped every provision, or that following a framework fulfils “Nothing About Us Without Us”.

The foundation helps teams apply established knowledge without repeatedly asking participants to rediscover known barriers. It cannot tell a team everything about the people using a particular service, their priorities, the circumstances of use or how an experience makes them feel. WCAG itself recognises that it does not address every user need. Standards and lived experience should inform one another. [4, 5]

Meaningful inclusion therefore requires current, inclusive research and a demonstrable opportunity for Deaf, disabled and neurodivergent people to influence project decisions. Their functional needs, emotional experiences, preferences, aspirations and disagreements belong within the evidence used to shape the whole service.

**Applying established guidance is the starting point. Including people in research, interpretation and decisions makes that foundation relevant to the project.**

## 3. Include people in ordinary research

Deaf, disabled and neurodivergent people are customers, colleagues, decision-makers and members of audiences. They have opinions about value, trust, language, aesthetics, convenience, quality and service priorities, as well as accessibility.

Recruiting them only for specialist accessibility studies positions them outside the assumed mainstream audience. Systematically leaving them out of ordinary research is a form of **othering**: treating their perspectives as a separate concern rather than part of the population the service exists to serve. The omission can distort findings even when researchers have no intention to exclude.

Every project using qualitative audience or customer research should include Deaf, disabled and neurodivergent participants within its recruitment plan. This applies to interviews, concept research, diary studies, co-design, message testing, brand research and usability sessions. Participants should be able to discuss the same substantive questions as everyone else, with accessible methods and appropriate flexibility.

Do not reinterpret everything a participant says as an accessibility issue. A Deaf participant's objection to pricing may be about pricing. A neurodivergent participant's preference for a brand may reflect its values. Ask what matters to the person and retain the context of their answer.

Specialist accessibility research remains valuable for exploring particular barriers or interactions in depth. It complements inclusive mainstream research.

### Proportional representation and deliberate breadth

All audience and customer research programmes should plan proportional inclusion of people who self-identify as Deaf, disabled or neurodivergent, using a relevant audience benchmark. Apply this differently according to the method:

| Research situation | Practical approach | Interpretation boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantitative audience or customer research | Set recruitment targets using credible, dated evidence about the intended audience; check accessible participation and non-response; obtain sufficient responses for planned comparisons | A quota alone does not establish representativeness. Explain sampling, weighting, uncertainty and coverage limitations |
| Small qualitative studies | Deliberately recruit a range of relevant lived experiences, functional needs and circumstances across the project's research rounds | Small samples cannot reproduce population proportions reliably or establish prevalence. Include people purposefully and document gaps |
| Research into a particular barrier or underserved group | Recruit additional participants with relevant experience, alongside evidence from other affected groups | Extra recruitment is appropriate for learning about an issue; it does not establish that group's population share |
| Services whose current customers reflect past exclusion | Consider intended customers, eligible non-users, people who abandoned the journey and people using alternatives | Current customer data may understate unmet demand and access barriers |

Use voluntary self-identification, allow overlapping identities and provide “prefer not to say”. Deaf identity, disability and neurodivergence are not mutually exclusive, and people use these terms differently. A person may have significant functional needs without identifying with any of them. Do not require diagnosis or medical proof.

Record the benchmark, definitions, recruitment rationale and achieved coverage. Where relevant evidence is uncertain, state the uncertainty and improve the plan. Do not invent a universal percentage or treat one participant as representing an entire community. Consider intersections such as age, language, ethnicity, income, digital confidence and multiple impairments where they affect the research question.

If recruitment falls short, change channels, provide access support, extend recruitment or add a research round. Record unresolved gaps and the decisions they limit; do not quietly remove inclusion from the plan.

## 4. Participation must have influence

Recruitment is necessary, but attendance alone does not establish meaningful participation. People need a realistic opportunity to shape what is investigated and what happens next.

1. **Set the questions together.** Involve affected people or appropriately constituted community advisers early enough to challenge assumptions, identify missing outcomes and influence priorities.
2. **Make participation accessible.** Offer accessible recruitment, consent and materials; suitable communication methods; interpreters or captions where needed; flexible timings, breaks and alternatives to live sessions. Ask about access arrangements without requiring a condition to be disclosed.
3. **Respect people's contribution.** Budget fair payment, access support and expenses. Explain the role, effort, influence and limits before participation. Do not depend on unpaid advocacy to make the project inclusive.
4. **Interpret findings with people.** Offer opportunities to challenge researchers' interpretations, especially where findings concern identity, dignity or emotional experience. Preserve different perspectives rather than manufacturing consensus.
5. **Show the effect.** Record what changed, what remains unresolved, who made the decision and why. Return an accessible account of the findings and resulting actions to participants who want it.

Participants contribute expertise about their own experiences. They are not responsible for certifying a service, representing everyone with a similar identity, or resolving technical defects already covered by established guidance. Community advisers and representative organisations add valuable perspectives, while customers and non-customers contribute different evidence about actual and potential use. Follow the existing framework's **Customers Versus Research Participants** discussion when interpreting those differences. [1]

Use established patterns and technical checks before evaluative sessions wherever possible. Early discovery and co-design should still involve people before a finished product exists; removing known defects from a prototype is different from postponing participation until the end.

## 5. Lived experience can reveal wider design opportunities

People with permanent conditions or impairments may have developed strategies, preferences and detailed knowledge that help teams recognise barriers in other circumstances. That expertise can expose design possibilities that a conventional sample misses.

This creates practical value as well as ethical value: richer evidence can improve decisions, reduce avoidable rework and reveal underserved demand. Inclusion can turn a commitment perceived as merely worthy into work that is demonstrably worthwhile. The rights and dignity of participants remain sufficient reasons to include them, whether or not a wider commercial benefit follows.

The examples below are **proposed research approaches**, not claims about completed studies or guaranteed outcomes.

| Design context | Lived experience to include | Opportunities to investigate | What still needs validation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Audio-only interaction, such as an Alexa skill | Congenitally blind people, alongside other blind users and people with varied audio-interface experience | Discoverability without visual cues, spoken structure, memory demands, orientation, confirmation and recovery | Sighted people using audio-only services may have different skills, expectations and environmental constraints |
| Important or life-changing decisions | Neurodivergent people with varied experiences, people with cognitive access needs and people who have faced the relevant decision | Clear options, manageable information, time to reflect, saved progress, reversible steps and support without loss of agency | Neurodivergence is not a proxy for distress or reduced capability; investigate the actual decision context |
| Travel and disrupted journeys | Disabled travellers, neurodivergent travellers and people managing fatigue or unfamiliar environments | Predictability, disruption information, accessible alternatives, low-effort recovery and continuity across channels | Noise, movement, connectivity and urgency require evidence from realistic travel contexts |
| Tax returns and forms with legal consequences | People with varied reading, memory, attention and executive-function needs, plus users with relevant experience | Plain-language explanations, consequences, error prevention, review, correction and confirmation | Domain knowledge and legal understanding differ from interaction skills; comprehension needs separate examination |
| Judicial and medical situations | People with relevant lived experience, including Deaf, disabled and neurodivergent participants | Communication choice, dignity, privacy, informed understanding, pacing, support and trust | Power imbalance, distress and sensitive disclosure require appropriate methods and support |
| Early morning, late evening or periods of low energy | People with fluctuating conditions, fatigue or relevant cognitive access needs | Resumability, reduced memory burden, flexible pace and recovery after interruption | Energy and cognition vary individually; time of day alone does not establish impairment |

Treat transfer as a hypothesis: identify the original barrier, the strategy that helps, the new context and the evidence needed to test it. A successful design for one group may introduce difficulty for another. Validate major changes across affected populations, as required by the existing framework's **Accessibility Evidence Should Never Come From A Single Group** section. [1]

Do not induce exhaustion, distress or unsafe distraction to reproduce a context. Use appropriately supported interviews, diaries, safe scenarios or observation chosen with participants. A disability simulation cannot replace lived experience.

## 6. Understand capability, capacity and context together

For this guidance, **capability** means what a person can do in the relevant interaction; **capacity** means the energy, attention, time and resources available to do it at that moment. These are working research terms, not clinical or legal assessments of capacity.

A person who can understand a complex service in a quiet setting may find it difficult while travelling, experiencing pain, managing medication effects, under emotional pressure or making a consequential decision. The service can create disabling demands through ambiguity, unnecessary complexity, urgency or lack of recovery options.

Explore the interaction between the person, the task and the circumstances. Record only context necessary for the research question. Do not assume that everyone is impaired early in the morning or late at night, that neurodivergent people have low capability, or that a diagnosis predicts preference or satisfaction.

Ask participants what they were trying to do, what resources they had available, what the service demanded and what would have helped. Keep the explanation grounded in evidence about that interaction.

## 7. Use functional segmentation without building disability profiles

The existing ethical framework recommends alignment with functional accessibility categories reflected in EAA Annex I. **Section VII's functional performance criteria** provide a useful starting point for checking research coverage. They are not a prescribed research segmentation scheme, a medical classification or a complete account of lived experience. [1, 6]

The following are concise research prompts based on its eleven areas; they do not reproduce the legal requirements:

| Section VII area, paraphrased | Research coverage prompt |
| --- | --- |
| Vision absent or limited; colour perception unavailable | Can people obtain and use information through the sensory modes available to them? |
| Hearing absent or limited | Can people follow communication through appropriate alternatives and audio options? |
| Vocal input unavailable | Can people participate without speech or other vocal input? |
| Manipulation, strength or reach limited | Can people interact without unsuitable physical demands? |
| Photosensitive seizure risk | Does evaluation avoid hazardous visual stimulation? |
| Cognitive demands | Can people understand, decide, remember and recover with suitable support? |
| Privacy when using accessibility features | Can people use access options without unnecessary disclosure? |

For detailed functional coverage, distinguish each applicable area within these grouped prompts. Add overlapping needs, communication language, task familiarity, emotional experience and context where relevant. People may belong to several functional segments and their needs may change.

This use of Section VII is a research design adaptation. The EAA gives those criteria a particular legal role; it does not prescribe the recruitment targets, satisfaction measures or segmentation proposed here. Inclusive research findings also do not establish legal conformity.

Keep three dimensions distinct: **self-described identity**, **functional need or preference**, and **context of use**. Collect each only when it serves a defined purpose. Do not infer identity or diagnosis from assistive technology, captions, navigation behaviour or accessibility settings. Refer to **Understanding Accessibility Signals** and **Disability Must Never Become a Justification for Surveillance** in the existing framework. [1]

## 8. Measure satisfaction in the context of the service

Task completion alone cannot establish equivalence. Investigate whether people receive the information they need, retain agency, expend reasonable effort and experience comparable dignity, confidence and satisfaction.

Use Double FW's emotional reframing of POUR to frame research questions. The first four feelings correspond to its interpretation of POUR; **feeling valued** is an additional proposed emotional principle, not a fifth WCAG principle. [3]

| Emotional outcome | Example question | Supporting evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feeling seen — Perceivable | “Was the information available in a form that worked for you?” | Information access, representation and missed content |
| Feeling capable — Operable | “Could you do what you intended, with the level of support you chose?” | Completion, effort, independence and recovery |
| Feeling confident — Understandable | “Did you understand your options, the consequences and what would happen next?” | Comprehension, uncertainty and confidence in decisions |
| Feeling safe — Robust | “Could you rely on the service and your chosen tools throughout?” | Reliability, continuity, privacy concerns and reported trust |
| Feeling valued — additional emotional principle | “Did you feel heard, respected and intentionally considered?” | Satisfaction, dignity, belonging and responsiveness |

Adapt questions with participants; these are prompts, not a validated psychometric scale. Ask open questions before introducing labels that might lead an answer. Investigate feeling heard through both the service experience and the response to research contributions.

Satisfaction must reflect the service's purpose. A tax return, court process or medical appointment should support clarity, dignity, agency and trust; an upsetting underlying outcome does not automatically mean the interface failed. Distinguish satisfaction with the process from satisfaction with the substantive outcome.

Examine outcomes by relevant functional need and context where sample size and privacy permit. Report missing responses and abandonment alongside completed journeys. An overall average can conceal exclusion, while small subgroup differences may be too uncertain to interpret. Combine quantitative results with qualitative explanations and record unresolved barriers. Positive satisfaction scores cannot justify avoidable harm, loss of rights or failure to meet applicable requirements.

## 9. Gather richer insight with minimum necessary data

Inclusive research does not require a permanent disability register. Apply the existing framework's collection, aggregation, consent, sharing and AI governance principles throughout. [1]

- Separate recruitment and access-arrangement details from research findings wherever practical. Restrict access to each according to purpose.
- Ask about functional needs and preferences directly when appropriate. Make identity disclosure optional and explain why any such question is asked.
- Explain participation, recording, withdrawal arrangements and their limits, retention, access and intended uses in accessible terms. Participation consent does not automatically authorise publication, AI training or unrelated reuse.
- Remove unnecessary identifiers early. Pseudonymisation does not make data anonymous; small groups, quotations and combinations of attributes can still identify someone.
- Publish or share insight at a safe level of aggregation. Combine or suppress risky breakdowns without implying that the affected experience did not exist.
- Do not use recordings, transcripts or sensitive responses in external AI tools or shared datasets without the permissions and safeguards required by the existing framework.

Where the CUSTOMER RESEARCH FRAMEWORK proposes personas, knowledge graphs, RAG records or training data, treat these as possible outputs subject to the ethical framework. A suggested output format is not permission to reuse participant data. Mark inferred personas, synthetic examples and hypotheses clearly; they are not observed testimony or substitutes for participants. [1, 2]

## 10. Turn inclusive evidence into evolving guidance

Guidance should evolve when better evidence reveals missing needs, failed assumptions or more effective approaches. Teams need a traceable path from research to change:

1. Record the observation and relevant context, including contradictory experiences and evidence limitations.
2. Review its interpretation with affected participants or advisers where appropriate.
3. Identify whether it concerns local implementation, a reusable pattern or a gap in guidance.
4. Assign a decision owner and agree an action, outcome measure and review point.
5. Validate the proposed change across relevant groups and contexts, retaining established technical checks.
6. Update local guidance, patterns or governance with a dated rationale and a privacy-safe evidence reference.
7. Explain the result to contributors and monitor the experience after implementation.

Research may justify strengthening guidance or proposing changes to external standards. It does not allow a local project to rewrite WCAG or waive applicable legal duties. Where evidence conflicts, record the trade-off and seek an inclusive solution rather than allowing the largest group's preference to erase a smaller group's needs.

Use the CUSTOMER RESEARCH FRAMEWORK's insight catalogue to connect findings to action. Add the following fields when useful, without storing identifiable participant detail in broadly accessible records. [2]

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Research question and service context | Explain the decision the evidence can inform |
| Recruitment coverage and gaps | Show whose perspectives were included and missing |
| Functional and emotional outcomes | Retain both ability to participate and quality of experience |
| Evidence reference and confidence | Distinguish observation, interpretation and hypothesis |
| Participant or adviser interpretation | Record agreement, disagreement and alternative explanations |
| Decision, owner and rationale | Make influence and accountability visible |
| Validation and guidance revision | Show whether the change worked and what learning was retained |

## 11. Project review checklist

Before treating research as sufficient evidence for a decision, check:

- [ ] Established guidance has informed the work, and its limits are understood.
- [ ] Mainstream research recruitment includes Deaf, disabled and neurodivergent people, even where accessibility is not the topic.
- [ ] Quantitative targets have a relevant benchmark; qualitative recruitment has a deliberate breadth rationale.
- [ ] Intended users and excluded non-users have been considered alongside current customers.
- [ ] Participation is accessible, voluntary, appropriately supported and fairly paid.
- [ ] People can influence questions, interpretations and decisions, with a clear account of what changed.
- [ ] Functional needs, identity and context are distinguished without unnecessary disclosure or profiling.
- [ ] Transferable insights have been tested in the proposed new context and across affected groups.
- [ ] Evidence covers informational and emotional equivalence, satisfaction and dignity as well as completion.
- [ ] Data collection, reuse, sharing and AI processing follow the existing ethical framework.
- [ ] Coverage gaps, uncertainty, disagreement and remaining barriers are explicit.
- [ ] Findings have an owner, an action and a route into improved guidance and future evaluation.

If evidence is insufficient, record what the project can reasonably conclude, what it cannot conclude and the further involvement required. Inclusion is demonstrated through participation that changes understanding and action, supported by ethical data practices and continuing accountability.
